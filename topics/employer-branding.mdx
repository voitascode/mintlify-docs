Every serious candidate visits your website before applying, and again before accepting. For a company of 80+ people competing for specialists, the careers section is doing real recruitment work — usually badly, and usually without anyone owning it.

## The careers page is a conversion page

It has a target audience, a decision to influence, and a measurable outcome. Everything from [conversion optimization](/topics/conversion-optimization) applies, with one difference: the visitor is deciding whether to make a significant change in their life, not whether to book a call.

That raises the bar on proof and honesty considerably.

## What candidates actually want to know

Not your values statement. In rough order of what determines whether someone applies:

<Columns cols={2}>
  <Card title="What the work actually is" icon="briefcase">
    Concrete daily reality — the stack, the team size, who they'd report to, what the first six months look like.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Whether they'd fit" icon="users">
    Real photos of real people, honest descriptions of how the team works, and what kind of person struggles here.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Money and arrangements" icon="wallet">
    Salary range, remote policy, hours. Omitting these filters out good candidates who won't gamble their time on a guess.
  </Card>

  <Card title="What happens next" icon="route">
    The hiring process, stage by stage, with a realistic timeline. Uncertainty loses candidates who have other options.
  </Card>
</Columns>

<Warning>
  Stock photos of a diverse team laughing at a laptop actively damage credibility. Candidates recognize them instantly, and the inference is that everything else on the page is equally generic.
</Warning>

## Honesty is a filter, and that's the point

The instinct is to present the job as uniformly excellent. This is counterproductive: it attracts people who leave within a year when reality arrives.

Saying plainly what's hard — the legacy system nobody's replaced, the fact that the role involves more meetings than making — filters out mismatches before they cost you a hire. Candidates who apply anyway are applying with their eyes open.

## Keep it current

An outdated careers page is worse than none. A listing for a role filled eight months ago tells candidates that nobody maintains this, which they'll reasonably generalize.

This needs an owner, like everything else in [web management](/topics/web-management) — usually shared between marketing and HR, which in practice means it belongs to neither unless someone says otherwise.

<Note>
  The cheapest improvement most companies can make: ask your three most recent hires what they wanted to know before applying and couldn't find. Then add exactly that.
</Note>
