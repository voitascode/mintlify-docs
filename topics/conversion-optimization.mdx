Conversion optimization has a reputation problem. It sounds like button colors and A/B tests, which is roughly the least useful version of it.

The useful version asks a simpler question: of the people who already arrive at your site and have the problem you solve, why do most of them leave without saying anything?

## Why traffic beats conversion in most budgets — and why that's backwards

If 2% of visitors enquire, doubling traffic doubles enquiries. So does moving conversion from 2% to 4% — and the second is usually cheaper, because you're improving something you already own rather than renting attention.

<Info>
  This is especially true in B2B, where traffic is expensive, considered, and often small. A site with 3,000 monthly visitors and a 1% conversion rate has more to gain from fixing the 99% than from chasing visitor 3,001.
</Info>

## Where B2B sites lose people

  <Card title="Unclear value" icon="help-circle">
    The visitor can't tell within seconds whether you solve their problem. Usually because the copy describes the company, not the customer's situation.
  </Card>

  <Card title="No credible proof" icon="badge-check">
    Claims without evidence. Named clients, real numbers, and specific outcomes beat adjectives.
  </Card>

  <Card title="High-friction forms" icon="form-input">
    Every optional field costs submissions. Ask for what you need to have the next conversation, not what the CRM would like.
  </Card>

  <Card title="One-size CTA" icon="mouse-pointer-click">
    "Book a demo" suits buyers who are ready. Everyone earlier needs a lower-commitment option, or they leave.
  </Card>
</Columns>

## Test what matters

If you do run tests, test things that could plausibly change behaviour: the offer, the headline, the form length, the page structure. Button colors and micro-copy rarely move B2B numbers enough to detect.

<Warning>
  Most B2B sites don't have the traffic for statistically valid A/B testing. With 3,000 monthly visitors, a test needs months to reach significance — by which point the season changed. Qualitative evidence (watching real users, reading enquiry emails) is often more reliable than underpowered tests.
</Warning>

## Measure the right thing

Form submissions are not the goal. Qualified enquiries are.

A form change that doubles submissions while halving quality has made the sales team's job worse. Track what happens *after* the form — how many became real conversations — or you'll optimize toward noise.
