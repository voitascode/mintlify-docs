The hardest part of running a company website usually isn't the website. It's the eleven people with opinions about it, one of whom signs off the budget.

This is the topic nobody trains marketers for, and it determines whether good work survives contact with the organization.

## Translate into their terms

Management doesn't fund websites. It funds outcomes it already cares about.

The second version isn't spin — it's the same problem expressed in the currency the decision-maker uses. Anything framed as taste competes with everyone else's taste. Anything framed as pipeline competes on evidence.

## Handling feedback from six directions

Every stakeholder reviews a website through their own lens: sales wants more form fields, legal wants more disclaimers, the CEO wants the logo bigger, and someone always wants a carousel.

Three things that help:

- **Separate opinion from requirement.** "I don't like the blue" is preference. "This must display our license number" is a constraint. Treat them differently and say which is which.
- **Route feedback to the goal.** "How does this help us get more qualified enquiries?" reframes discussion from taste to purpose without dismissing anyone.
- **Collect feedback in one place, once.** Six separate email threads produce contradictory instructions that you get blamed for reconciling.

<Warning>
  The most damaging pattern is the late-arriving senior opinion — someone uninvolved for eight weeks who requests fundamental changes at handover. Prevent it by getting them into the approval at step two, not by hoping they stay quiet.
</Warning>

## Make the invisible visible

Much of website work is invisible when it goes well. Nobody notices the site didn't get hacked, the backups worked, or the page loads quickly.

This is a structural problem: your successes are absences. Counter it by reporting proactively — a short monthly note on enquiries, traffic quality, and what changed. It builds the credibility you'll need when you ask for budget, and it makes your work legible before something goes wrong.

## When you're overruled

Sometimes the decision goes against the evidence. Document the recommendation and the reasoning once, without drama, and implement what was decided.

This isn't cynicism. It's how you stay credible: you were on record, you didn't sabotage the outcome, and the next recommendation carries more weight.
