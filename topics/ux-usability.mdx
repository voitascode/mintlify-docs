Most usability problems on B2B websites aren't subtle. They're not discovered through eye-tracking studies or elaborate testing programmes. They're the kind of thing five minutes of watching a real person use the site would reveal immediately — which is precisely why they survive: nobody ever watches.

## The gap between you and your visitor

You know what your company does. You know what the products are called internally, why the navigation is organized that way, and which page has the thing people ask about.

Your visitor knows none of this. They arrived from a search result, they have a specific question, and they'll give you somewhere between ten and thirty seconds to demonstrate that the answer is here.

<Warning>
  The most expensive usability failure in B2B is naming things the way your company thinks about them rather than the way buyers do. Nobody searches for your internal product taxonomy.
</Warning>

## Testing without a budget

You do not need a research programme. You need five people and an hour.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find five people who aren't you">
    Ideally similar to real buyers. Failing that, anyone outside your team — a friend in another industry works better than a colleague who already knows the answer.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Give them a real task">
    Not "have a look around." Something concrete: "find out whether this company could help with X, and how you'd get in touch."
  </Step>

  <Step title="Watch, and stay quiet">
    This is the hard part. Don't explain, don't hint, don't defend. Every time you want to say "it's under that menu," write it down instead — that's a finding.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Fix what all five hit">
    If everyone stumbles in the same place, that's not preference, it's a defect. Individual quirks can wait.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Five people will find the large majority of your serious problems. The sixth mostly repeats the first five.

## The usual suspects

Patterns that turn up in nearly every B2B site audit:

- **Navigation named after the org chart** — "Solutions" and "Services" as separate menus, meaningful only internally
- **No clear next step** — pages that inform and then stop, with no obvious action
- **Contact hidden behind a form** with eleven required fields, three of which nobody wants to answer at that stage
- **Mobile as an afterthought** — despite mobile being where a large share of first visits happen
- **Pricing that doesn't exist anywhere**, forcing a sales call for basic qualification, which many buyers simply won't do

## Usability and conversion are the same problem

A visitor who can't find what they need doesn't convert. This is why usability work usually pays for itself faster than a redesign — you're removing friction from traffic you've already earned rather than buying more.
